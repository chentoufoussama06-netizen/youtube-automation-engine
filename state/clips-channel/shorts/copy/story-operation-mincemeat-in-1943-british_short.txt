In 1943, Operation Mincemeat used a dead body to fool the Nazis and disguise the Allied invasion of Sicily. The Allies planned to invade Sicily, but Winston Churchill knew the location was too obvious. To deceive the enemy, British intelligence officers Ewen Montagu and Charles Cholmondeley designed a daring plan based on a 1939 memo by Rear Admiral John Godfrey and Ian Fleming. They obtained the body of Glyndwr Michael, a homeless man who died from eating rat poison. They dressed him as a Royal Marines officer, Captain William Martin, and placed personal items on him. They also attached fake correspondence between two British generals suggesting the Allies planned to invade Greece and Sardinia instead of Sicily. To keep the body from freezing, which would look suspicious, the mortuary kept it at four degrees Celsius. Churchill approved the plan, joking that if the Spaniards returned the body with the papers unread, they would just have to give it another swim. Eisenhower gave the final confirmation on April 17, 1943. A submarine transported the body to the southern coast of Spain and released it. On April 30, 1943, a Spanish fisherman found the corpse. To make the deception believable, the British sent pre-scripted, coded diplomatic cables demanding the return of the briefcase, knowing the Germans had broken their code. The nominally neutral Spanish government shared copies of the secret documents with the German military intelligence organization, the Abwehr, before returning the originals. Forensic examination proved the papers had been read, and Ultra decrypts of German messages confirmed the Germans fell for the ruse. The deception worked perfectly. German reinforcements shifted to Greece and Sardinia before and during the invasion. Sicily received none. Grand Admiral Karl Dönitz even met with Adolf Hitler to discuss the documents. Decades later, Montagu wrote the bestselling book *The Man Who Never Was* to tell the story of the operation.
