Byzantine ships possessed a terrifying weapon that could burn directly on open water. Greek fire allowed the imperial fleet to repel invading Arab forces by the late seventh century, but how did this liquid burn afloat? Most modern scholars agree the Byzantines did not rely on quicklime, which fails to ignite in the open sea. Instead, Alexandrian chemical knowledge evolved into a crude form of modern napalm. Chemists mixed a petroleum base with resins, creating an adhesive liquid that burned without needing contact with water. Byzantine sailors loaded this mixture into small hand-thrown grenades, or pumped it forward through heavy tubes called siphōns, spraying hot flames directly onto target decks. Arab fleets captured intact ships and tubes, but they could never master the specialized siphōn deployment system. The formula remained a closely guarded state secret until it was lost to history. Greek fire never made the Byzantine navy invincible, but it stopped the fall of Constantinople and protected the empire for centuries.
