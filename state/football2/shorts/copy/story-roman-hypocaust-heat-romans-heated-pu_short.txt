But how did this ancient system actually work without burning down the rooms above? First, the floor of the hypocaust was raised above the ground by pillars called pilae stacks. These stacks supported a layer of tiles, followed by a layer of concrete, and finally the floor tiles of the rooms above. Next, hot air and smoke from the furnace circulated through this enclosed area. Then, the air moved up through clay or tile flues in the walls—referred to as caliducts—to outlets in the roof. Rooms intended to be the warmest were located nearest to the furnace below. Finally, the heat output was regulated by adjusting the amount of wood fed to the fire. Because it required constant attention to the fire and a lot of fuel, it was expensive and labour-intensive to run. Yet, this technology improved the hygiene and living conditions of citizens, serving as a direct forerunner of modern central heating.
