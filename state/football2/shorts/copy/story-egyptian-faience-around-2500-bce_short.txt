Eighty-five inches. That is how long a single Egyptian faience sceptre measures. How did this non-clay ceramic, known as Egyptian faience or *tjehenet*, transform crushed stone into a dazzling glaze that imitated precious lapis lazuli and turquoise? The secret lay in an artificial medium made of crushed quartz or sand, mixed with calcite lime, water-soluble alkali salts, and copper mineral pigments. Artisans modeled, molded, or threw this powdered mixture to shape everything from beads and dishware to massive sceptres over two meters long. During the self-glazing efflorescence process, water inside the core evaporated, drawing the alkali salts to the surface where they recrystallized into a thin coating. When fired, partial heating caused the quartz grains to fuse together while the surface underwent vitrification. The copper compounds melted into a bright, glassy blue-green luster smooth against the skin. Later, artisans buried quartz objects in flux-rich glazing powders inside heated vessels, forcing the flux to migrate and form a vitreous shell. Faience became humanity's first high-technology ceramic.
