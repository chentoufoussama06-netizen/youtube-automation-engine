Mesopotamian cities built massive terraced towers that were not for public worship. These were ziggurats, and ordinary citizens were strictly forbidden from entering them. The ancient Mesopotamians built these structures to connect heaven and earth, believing their patron gods lived at the top. To keep the gods there, priests offered music, harvested produce, and devotional statues. Constructing a ziggurat required building a mastaba-like structure with a flat top, rising between two and seven terraced stories. Each step was built slightly smaller than the level below it. Workers constructed the core using sun-baked bricks, then encased the exterior with fired bricks. These outer facings were often glazed in different colors for astrological significance, and kings sometimes engraved their names directly into them. On one side, series of ramps or a spiral staircase allowed access to the summit. Functionally, the height allowed priests to escape annual floods and guard sacred rituals against non-priests. Today, the ziggurat shape has experienced a modern revival, inspiring structures like the Al Zaqura Building in Baghdad.
