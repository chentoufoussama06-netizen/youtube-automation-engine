Modern concrete breaks down in saltwater within decades. Yet two-thousand-year-old Roman concrete harbour structures remain intact after centuries of exposure to harsh marine environments. How did ancient builders make a material so unusually durable? It began with hydraulic mortar. Around 25 BC, Vitruvius recommended pozzolana—a volcanic sand high in alumina and silica from Naples or Rome. For underwater work, he specified a ratio of one part lime to two parts pozzolana. When seawater percolated through tiny cracks in this marine concrete, it reacted with phillipsite in the volcanic rock and quicklime. This chemical reaction grew rare aluminous tobermorite crystals that helped resist fracturing. Research in 2023 uncovered a second mechanism. Hot-mixing with quicklime created brittle lime clasts. When water seeped into new cracks, it reacted with these clasts to produce reactive calcium, forming new calcium carbonate crystals that resealed the fractures. Today, modern builders are exploring Roman techniques by replacing volcanic ash with coal fly ash. This process can reduce concrete costs by up to 60 percent while lowering carbon dioxide emissions.
